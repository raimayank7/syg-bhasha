Reading kanji: on readings, kun readings and how to choose

A kanji has one meaning (or a few) but often several readings. That is the main reason kanji take time. This guide explains the system, then explains the data on each page of this site.

Two families of readings

  • On readings (on'yomi) are based on the Chinese pronunciation that came with the character. They are usually short and often used when a kanji is part of a compound made of kanji only. In dictionaries they are written in katakana.
  • Kun readings (kun'yomi) are the Japanese words that the character was used to write. They are often used when the kanji stands alone or is followed by kana. In dictionaries they are written in hiragana.

Okurigana and the dot

The kana that follow a kanji in a verb or an adjective are called okurigana. In the dictionary data, a dot marks where the kanji part ends: a reading written あ.げる means that あ is read for the kanji and げる is written after it in kana. A hyphen marks a reading that only appears as a prefix or a suffix.

Choosing a reading

There is no simple rule. Compounds of several kanji usually use on readings, a single kanji with okurigana usually uses a kun reading, and there are many exceptions. Learners pick up the reading together with the word, so learn whole words rather than readings alone.

What the data on each page means

  • Grade is the school year in which the kanji is taught in Japan.
  • Strokes is the number of strokes in one standard form.
  • Radical is the number of the classical Kangxi radical used to find the kanji in a dictionary. The radical shown is the standalone radical character, which may look a little different from the shape inside the kanji.
  • Frequency rank is a position in a list of the 2,500 kanji most used in newspapers, from the dictionary file. A low number means common.
  • Old JLPT level is a four-level scale from before 2010, where 1 is the hardest. The test now has five levels and no official kanji list, so this is not the same as N1 to N5.

What is not here

The data has no stroke order, no example words and no sentences, so this site cannot teach those. A school book, a teacher or a good dictionary can.

Questions

Do I need to learn every reading?

Not at first. Learn the common ones with whole words, and meet the rest as you read.

What does the dot in a kun reading mean?

It separates the part read for the kanji from the okurigana, the kana written after it.
