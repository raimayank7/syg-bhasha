Kanji 丁

street, ward, town

丁

丁 is a kanji taught in grade 3 of Japanese elementary school. It is written with 2 strokes. In KANJIDIC2 its English meaning is street, ward, town, counter for guns, tools, leaves or cakes of something, even number.

Its classical radical is number 1 (⼀, "One"), the part that dictionaries use to look it up. Other grade 3 kanji with the same radical: 世, 両.

It is number 1312 in a ranking of the 2,500 kanji most used in newspapers. KANJIDIC2 puts it at level 1 of the old four-level JLPT scale (1 is the hardest). Both are statistics from the file; see reading kanji for how to use them.

Among grade 3 kanji

Grade 3 has 200 kanji; 1 of them (including this one) have 2 strokes, which is fewer than the middle of the grade. It is the only one in its grade with that number of strokes.

Readings

On readings (from Chinese) are in katakana; kun readings (Japanese) are in hiragana, with a dot before the part that is written after the kanji (okurigana). The small letters are romaji.

On readings

  • チョウchou
  • テイtei
  • チンchin
  • トウtou
  • チchi

Kun readings

  • ひのとhinoto

How to write its readings

KANJIDIC2 lists 5 on readings and 1 kun reading for 丁. On readings come from old Chinese and are mostly heard when the kanji sits inside a word of two or more kanji. Kun readings are native Japanese words, heard when the kanji stands almost alone, often followed by kana. Which reading a given word uses cannot be guessed from the kanji alone; you learn it together with the word.

This is how the first kun readings turn into writing:

  • ひのと (hinoto): no okurigana is marked, so the kanji 丁 alone writes it.

Kanji with the same sound

Japanese has many kanji that share a reading. Among the 1,026 elementary kanji, 12 others begin with the on reading chou (チョウ): 兆 (portent); 帳 (notebook); 庁 (government office); 張 (counter for bows & stringed instruments); 朝 (morning); 潮 (tide); 町 (town); 腸 (intestines); 調 (tune); 長 (long). Heard without the written form, only the context tells you which one is meant.

Its radical family

Radical 1 is shared by 16 jōyō kanji in the file: 5 in grade 1, 1 in grade 2, 3 in grade 3, 1 in grade 4, 1 in grade 6, 5 in secondary school. Kanji with one radical often have something in common in meaning, so learning them together can help memory, but the link is loose and there are many exceptions.

How hard is it to write?

With 2 strokes, 丁 has more strokes than 1 of the 1,026 elementary kanji and fewer than 1015. Stroke count is only one side of difficulty: shape, look-alike kanji and the number of readings matter too. This site does not show stroke order, so check it in a school book or a good dictionary before you practise.
