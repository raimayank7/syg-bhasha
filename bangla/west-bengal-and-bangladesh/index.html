Bangla in West Bengal and Bangladesh: everyday word differences

Bangla is one language with one script and one literature, shared by two countries. A reader in Kolkata and a reader in Dhaka read the same poems and understand each other without effort. But everyday speech on the two sides, and among different communities on each side, prefers different words for a number of common things. A learner should know both sets, because you will hear both.

The difference is mostly one of source. For some everyday ideas, speech in West Bengal tends to use a word of Sanskrit origin, while speech in Bangladesh, and among many Muslim speakers in India too, tends to use a word of Persian or Arabic origin. A Hindi speaker knows this pattern well from Hindi and Urdu: जल and पानी, स्नान and ग़ुस्ल.

How to read this table

  • "More usual" does not mean "only". Both words of each pair are understood on both sides, and many people use either according to company. A Muslim family in Kolkata may say pani and khala; a Hindu family in Dhaka may say jol and mashi. The choice follows community and habit as much as the border.
  • The grammar, the script and the great bulk of the vocabulary are the same. The lists of pairs that people enjoy quoting are short compared with the shared language.
  • Neither set is more correct. As a learner, use the words of the people you are speaking with. If you do not know, either will do.

Differences of sound

The standard written language is based on the speech of the region around Kolkata and Nadia, and educated speech in Dhaka is close to it. In everyday talk, accents differ, and Bangla has many regional forms, such as those of Sylhet and Chittagong, that can be hard even for other Bengalis to follow. One small thing a learner may notice: verb forms such as "I am doing" are করছি (korchhi) in standard speech, and often করতেছি (kortechhi) in the colloquial speech of Bangladesh.

Spelling and numbers

The alphabet, the digits and the rules of spelling are shared. A few spellings of foreign words differ in practice, and each side has its own language academy that publishes spelling guidance, so you may see small variations in newspapers, such as different choices between the long and short i.

What this site uses

The phrases on this site are in standard colloquial Bangla as spoken in West Bengal, since most of our readers are in India, and both words are shown wherever the difference matters, as with water, twenty and the family words.

This page is a practical guide for learners, based on widely known usage. Speech habits are personal and local; treat the table as a general picture, not a rule about any person or community.
