Korean speech levels: formal, polite and casual

Korean verbs change their endings to show how you feel about the person you are talking to. There are several levels, but a beginner needs only three.

The three levels you need first

LevelEndingUse
Formal polite-ㅂ니다 / -습니다News, speeches, meetings, customer service, first meetings with elders
Informal polite-아요 / -어요Everyday speech with people you do not know well or who are older
Casual-아 / -어Close friends of the same age, and younger people

The same sentence in three levels

MeaningFormalInformal politeCasual
Thank you감사합니다고마워요고마워
(I) eat먹습니다먹어요먹어
(I) go갑니다가요가
(I) am a student학생입니다학생이에요학생이야

Which one to choose

  • When in doubt, use the informal polite level (-요). It is polite without being stiff.
  • Use the formal level in public speaking, in writing for an audience and with senior people at work.
  • Use the casual level only when the other person has invited it or is clearly a close friend or a child. Using it with a stranger or an elder can seem rude.

The honorific -시-

To show respect to the person you are talking about (not only the person you speak to), Korean adds -시- to the verb: 가요 (go) becomes 가세요, 먹어요 (eat) becomes 드세요 (a special honorific verb is used for "eat" and a few other verbs). The greeting 안녕하세요 is built from this pattern.

Words change too

Some words have honorific forms: 나이 (age) becomes 연세, 집 (house) becomes 댁. Learners usually pick these up gradually.

A note on this page

The examples are standard textbook sentences, and no native speaker has read this page. Rules about speech levels have many fine points, and usage differs by region, age and situation.

Questions

Do I need to learn all the levels?

No. Start with the informal polite (-요) and the formal (-ㅂ니다/-습니다) levels. Add the casual level when you have Korean friends.

Is it rude to use the wrong level?

It can be, especially using the casual level with someone older or unfamiliar. Koreans are usually forgiving of learners, but it is better to err on the side of being polite.
