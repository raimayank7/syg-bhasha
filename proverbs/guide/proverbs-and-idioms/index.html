Proverbs, idioms and sayings: what is the difference?

People often use "proverb", "idiom" and "saying" as if they meant the same thing. They overlap, but there is a useful difference.

A proverb

A proverb is a complete short sentence that gives advice or states a general truth: practice makes perfect. You can say it on its own and it makes sense.

An idiom

An idiom is a phrase whose meaning is not what the words say, and it usually fits into a sentence: kick the bucket (to die), spill the beans (to reveal a secret). An idiom is not advice.

A cliché

A cliché is any phrase that has been used so often that it has lost its force, such as "at the end of the day". Some proverbs and idioms become clichés.

A quotation

A quotation has a known author, such as "knowledge is power" is often credited to Francis Bacon, but it is also used as an everyday saying.

A simple test

  • Does it give advice or state a truth as a whole sentence? It is probably a proverb.
  • Does it mean something other than its words, and fit inside a sentence? It is an idiom.
  • Is it tired from overuse? It is a cliché.

The sayings on this site are proverbs. The idioms of English have their own pages elsewhere in the ShareYourGroup family.

Questions

Is "break a leg" a proverb?

No. It is an idiom: a phrase meaning "good luck" that does not say that in its words.

Can a proverb be an idiom?

Some proverbs contain idiomatic phrases, but a proverb as a whole gives advice, and an idiom does not.
