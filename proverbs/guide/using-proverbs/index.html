Using proverbs well in speech and writing

A proverb is a short saying that carries advice or an observation about life. A well-chosen one can sum up a point in a few words. A badly chosen one sounds tired or even rude. These guidelines help.

Choose one that fits the point

Do not pick a proverb because it sounds good. Ask: does it say what I mean? "A stitch in time saves nine" fits fixing a small problem early, not saving money. If the match is loose, skip it.

Use it once

One proverb in a paragraph is enough. Two or three together sound like a list of quotations and hide your own thinking.

Introduce it lightly

  • "As the saying goes, ..."
  • "They say that ..."
  • Or just state it and move on, trusting the reader to know it.

Do not use a proverb to end an argument

Telling someone "better safe than sorry" when they are worried is not an answer. Proverbs summarise common wisdom, but they do not prove anything, and many proverbs contradict each other (look before you leap, and strike while the iron is hot). Use them to illustrate an idea, not to win it.

Be careful with clichés in essays

The commonest proverbs are so familiar that readers skim them. In an essay or a speech, explain the idea in your own words and use the proverb to name it, not to replace your thought.

Kindness

Some sayings can sound like criticism ("pride comes before a fall", "a fool and his money are soon parted"). Do not use them against a person in front of others.

Questions

Is it fine to change the wording of a proverb?

People do vary them (for example "a bad workman blames his tools" and "a bad workman always blames his tools"), and a playful twist can work, but keep the standard form when you are teaching or writing formally.

Are proverbs always true?

No. They are common wisdom, and many contradict each other. Use them as a way to express an idea, not as proof.
