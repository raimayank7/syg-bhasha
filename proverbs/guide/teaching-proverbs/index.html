Teaching proverbs to children and learners of English

Proverbs are good teaching material: they are short, rhythmic and about everyday life. They are also hard, because the meaning is not in the words. Here are classroom and home ideas that work.

1. Start with a picture and a story

Choose one proverb and tell a two-line story that shows it. For "a stitch in time saves nine": a boy sees a small tear in his bag and mends it at once. Later, his friend ignores a tear and loses his books. Ask: what is the lesson?

2. Match the halves

Cut each proverb into two halves ("practice makes" / "perfect"). Learners match them. Then ask what each means.

3. Act it out

Children act a short scene without words, and others guess which proverb it shows.

4. Compare with the home language

Ask learners for a saying in their own language with a similar idea, and compare. The meanings often match even when the images differ. Do not claim that a proverb "means exactly the same": say that the idea is similar.

5. Write an example sentence

Each learner writes a sentence like the ones on the saying pages, using the proverb at the end. Check that the meaning fits.

What to avoid

  • Do not ask learners to memorise a long list without meaning.
  • Do not teach proverbs that are unkind or out of date without discussing them.

Questions

How many proverbs should I teach at once?

Two or three a week, with meanings and a short story each, is enough. More than that and they blur together.

Should learners memorise the exact wording?

Aim for the standard wording for the common ones, but understanding the meaning matters more.
