Subject-verb agreement: rules and tricky cases

A verb must agree with its subject: a singular subject takes a singular verb, a plural subject a plural verb. In the present simple this means the -s on the verb with he, she, it and singular nouns: She works, They work.

Cases that cause mistakes

  • Words between the subject and the verb do not change it. "The box of apples is on the table." (the subject is box, not apples)
  • Each, every, everyone, someone, nobody take a singular verb: "Everyone is here." "Each of the boys has a bat."
  • Either / neither usually take a singular verb: "Neither of them was late."
  • With "and" the subject is plural: "Tea and coffee are ready." But when the two things are one idea, the verb is singular: "Bread and butter is my breakfast."
  • With "or", "nor", "either...or", "neither...nor" the verb agrees with the nearer subject: "Neither the teacher nor the students were ready."
  • "As well as", "with", "along with" do not make the subject plural: "The teacher, along with her students, is coming."
  • Collective nouns such as team, family, government take a singular verb when the group acts as one ("The team is winning") and a plural verb in British English when the members act separately ("The team are arguing").
  • Words that look plural but are singular: news, mathematics, physics, economics: "The news is good."
  • Words that are always plural: scissors, trousers, jeans, glasses: "My trousers are new." (a pair of trousers is ...)
  • Amounts and distances are usually singular: "Ten rupees is not enough." "Five kilometres is a long way."

Check yourself

  1. The list of names (is / are) long. Answer: is.
  2. Neither my brother nor my sisters (was / were) at home. Answer: were.
  3. Physics (is / are) my favourite subject. Answer: is.
  4. Each of the girls (has / have) a prize. Answer: has.

Questions

Is "the team is" or "the team are" correct?

Both occur. American English uses "is" for a team acting as one. British English also accepts "are" when the members are thought of one by one.

Is "none of them is" or "none of them are" correct?

Both are used. "None are" is common in speech, and "none is" is the stricter form in formal writing.
