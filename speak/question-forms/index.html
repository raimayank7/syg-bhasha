How to ask questions in English

Half of every conversation is questions, and questions are where Hindi and Punjabi speakers slip most, because in our languages the voice alone can turn a sentence into a question. English needs a change in word order. There are only four patterns to learn.

Pattern 1: Yes or no questions

Put the helping verb before the doer.

StatementQuestion
You are ready.Are you ready?
She can swim.Can she swim?
They have eaten.Have they eaten?
He will come.Will he come?

If the statement has no helping verb, borrow do, does or did:

  • You like tea. → Do you like tea?
  • He lives here. → Does he live here? (the "s" moves to "does")
  • They came late. → Did they come late? (the past moves to "did")

Answer with a short form, not a bare yes or no: "Yes, I do." "No, he doesn't." "Yes, they did."

Pattern 2: Wh-questions

Question word + helping verb + doer + main verb. The Hindi "क" words map neatly:

HindiEnglishExample
क्याWhatWhat are you doing?
कहाँWhereWhere do you live?
कबWhenWhen does the bus leave?
क्योंWhyWhy did he leave early?
कौनWhoWho is at the door?
कैसेHowHow did you come?
कौन-साWhichWhich colour do you like?
किसकाWhoseWhose bag is this?
कितना (दाम, मात्रा)How muchHow much is this?
कितने (गिनती)How manyHow many people came?

The common error is to keep statement order: "Where you are going?" The helper must come before the doer: "Where are you going?"

One exception: when "who" or "what" is itself the doer, no helper is added: "Who called you?" "What happened?"

Pattern 3: Polite questions

To a stranger, a senior or a customer, a direct question can sound sharp. Wrap it:

  • Could you tell me where the station is? (not "where is the station" inside the wrap; the order goes back to normal)
  • Do you know what time the bank opens?
  • May I ask how old the building is?
  • Would you mind telling me your name?

Pattern 4: Question tags

A tag is the little question at the end that asks the listener to agree: "है न?" Hindi has one tag for everything. English matches the tag to the sentence, and flips positive to negative.

  • It is hot, isn't it?
  • You are coming, aren't you?
  • She can drive, can't she?
  • He does not eat meat, does he?
  • They went home, didn't they?

"Isn't it?" is right only after "It is...". Using it after everything ("You are going, isn't it?") is one of the best-known marks of Indian English.

Twenty questions to practise today

Ask a family member these, and make them ask you: What time did you get up? What did you have for breakfast? Where are you going today? Who are you going to meet? How will you go? When will you be back? Have you had lunch? Did you sleep well? Do you like this weather? Can you help me this evening? Which programme are you watching? Why are you laughing? How much did this cost? How many chapattis shall I make? Whose turn is it? Is the water hot? Are you free tomorrow? Will you come with me? Could you pass the salt? You are tired, aren't you?
