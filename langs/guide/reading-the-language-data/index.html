Reading the language data: percentages, scripts and limits

The pages on this site use one source, the Unicode CLDR locale data, which phones and computers use to show dates, numbers and names in the right language. This guide explains the labels so that you do not read more into them than they say.

Share of people

For each country, CLDR keeps an estimate of the share of the population that uses each language. It is a "population percent" for the language in that place. It is not a count of native speakers: it may include people who use a language as a second language, and it is rounded. Because many people use more than one language, the shares in a country can add up to more than 100%.

Official status

"Official" means the language is an official language of the country. "Official in a region" means it is official in part of the country (for example in some states of India), not everywhere. "Official in practice" is CLDR's label for a language that is used as official without a formal law. "Not official" means CLDR does not record it as official, which says nothing about how important it is to its speakers.

Script and direction

A script is the writing system, such as Devanagari, Arabic or Latin. One language can be written in more than one script (Punjabi in Gurmukhi in India and Shahmukhi in Pakistan, for example), so we show the usual script in the CLDR "likely" data. Direction is the way lines run: most scripts run left to right, while Arabic, Hebrew and some others run right to left.

Days, months, dates and numbers

The names of the days and months, the way a date is written and the way a number is grouped are taken from the language's own locale data. They show how software written for that language prints them, and they can differ from older traditional usage. In some languages digits of another system (such as Devanagari digits) are the default; the page says so.

What the data cannot tell you

  • How many people speak a language in total. CLDR does not give one number and we do not invent one.
  • Whether a language is endangered, or where it comes from. That is a question for linguists and for the community.
  • Whether something is a language or a dialect. The list follows CLDR and takes no side.
  • Small languages. The list holds languages with locale data, which leaves out many.

Questions

Is the percentage the number of speakers?

No. It is CLDR's rough estimate of the share of a country's people who use the language, which may include second-language use.

Why does a language have two scripts?

Some languages are written in more than one script in different places. The page shows the usual one in CLDR.
