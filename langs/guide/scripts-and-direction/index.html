Writing systems and direction: scripts, alphabets and abugidas

A script is a set of written signs, and a language is something people speak or sign. The two are separate, which is why many languages share a script and a few languages use more than one.

Kinds of script

  • Alphabets have a sign for each consonant and vowel. Latin, Greek and Cyrillic are alphabets.
  • Abjads mainly write consonants and leave most vowels to the reader or add them as marks. Arabic and Hebrew are abjads.
  • Abugidas write a consonant with a built-in vowel and change the vowel with a sign. Devanagari (used for Hindi, Marathi and Nepali), Bengali, Gurmukhi (Punjabi), Tamil and Thai are abugidas.
  • Syllabaries have a sign for each syllable. Japanese kana is one.
  • Logographic systems use signs for words or word parts. Chinese characters are the main example, and Japanese and Korean have also used them.

Direction

Most scripts run from left to right. Arabic, Hebrew, Syriac and Thaana run from right to left, and numbers inside such text are still written left to right. Traditional Chinese, Japanese and Korean can also be written in columns. Software needs special care to mix directions in one line, which is why a name in Latin letters inside an Arabic sentence can look odd in a badly made app.

One script, many languages

Latin is used for hundreds of languages, Arabic for Arabic, Persian, Urdu, Pashto and others, Devanagari for Hindi, Marathi, Nepali and more. The letters may be extended with extra signs for each language's sounds.

One language, more than one script

Punjabi is written in Gurmukhi in India and in Shahmukhi (an Arabic-based script) in Pakistan. Serbian is written in both Cyrillic and Latin. Software has to know which one the user wants, which is part of why locale data lists scripts.

Seeing it in practice

Open any language page and look at the days of the week: the same seven days appear in the script of the language, with the English name beside it. The compare tool shows two languages at once.

Questions

Is Hindi written in the same script as Sanskrit?

Yes, both are usually written in Devanagari, which is why they look alike on the page though the languages differ.

Why do some languages run right to left?

It is a feature of the script, not the language: Arabic-based and Hebrew scripts developed that way.
