ISL courses and careers

If you want to work with Indian Sign Language, not only use it, there are formal qualifications. This page describes the two diplomas offered by ISLRTC as its website describes them, read on 12 November 2026. Seats, eligibility, fees and dates are decided each year: always check the current admission notice on islrtc.nic.in before you plan.

DISLI: Diploma in Indian Sign Language Interpretation

  • What it is for: training interpreters. ISLRTC says the diploma "enables the trainees to acquire knowledge, skill and competencies in interpreting Indian Sign Language into other verbal languages and vice versa", in the classroom and in real life.
  • Approval: the page states that the course is approved by RCI, the Rehabilitation Council of India.
  • Duration: 2 years.
  • Intake capacity at ISLRTC: 90, as shown on the page.
  • Syllabus: published on the DISLI page.

DTISL: Diploma in Teaching Indian Sign Language

  • What it is for: ISLRTC says the course "is meant for training deaf individuals to teach ISL to interpreters and other stakeholders". Trainees learn to teach ISL as a language and to teach interpretation skills, and learn about Deaf culture and identity.
  • Approval: the page says RCI approved the programme in 2018, with the first batch for 2019-21.
  • Duration: 2 years.
  • Intake capacity at ISLRTC: 120, as shown on the page.
  • Syllabus: published on the DTISL page.

Other institutes approved by RCI may also run these diplomas. RCI's own website lists approved institutions.

What an interpreter does

An interpreter stands between a Deaf person and a hearing person and carries meaning both ways: from speech into ISL and from ISL into speech. It is skilled, tiring work that needs fluency in two languages, a good memory and strict ethics.

  • Education: interpreting lessons and lectures for Deaf students. ISLRTC publishes a "Do's and Don'ts for Educational Interpreting" document under Resources.
  • Public services: hospitals, courts, police stations, banks and government offices.
  • Media and events: news bulletins, public addresses, conferences and functions.
  • Remote interpreting: by video call. ISLRTC's site lists a Video Relay Service.

Is there a need?

ISLRTC's About page says that interpreters "are an urgent requirement at institutes and places where communication between deaf and hearing people takes place" and that India had "only less than 300 certified interpreters" when that text was written. ISLRTC keeps a directory of ISL interpreters. We do not state salaries here: they depend on the employer and we have no official figure.

Ethics of the work

  • Confidentiality. What is said in a doctor's room or a lawyer's office stays there.
  • Accuracy. The interpreter adds nothing, leaves nothing out and gives no opinion.
  • Respect for the Deaf person's choices. The interpreter is a bridge, not a guardian.
  • Knowing one's limits. A beginner should not accept legal or medical assignments.

Other ways to work with ISL

  • Teaching Deaf children as a special educator, with ISL as a working language.
  • Teaching ISL, a role in which Deaf teachers lead.
  • Making accessible content: ISL versions of videos, textbooks and public information.
  • Research on the language, in linguistics departments.
  • Any public-facing job, where basic ISL is a real advantage: banks, railways, airlines, hospitals, retail.

Before you apply

Take the free basic course first and spend time with Deaf signers. You will know quickly whether this is work you love. Then read the current prospectus on the official site. Beware of private "certificates" that claim to make you an interpreter in a few weeks.
