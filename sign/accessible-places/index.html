Making a place accessible to Deaf people

Most of what shuts Deaf people out of public life is not unkindness. It is information that exists only as sound: the announcement, the name called out in a waiting room, the bell, the person at the counter who speaks while looking down. Changing this costs little. Here is a checklist you can act on this month.

Any counter or reception

  • Keep a notepad and pen on the counter, or a tablet with a notes app.
  • Display a token number screen instead of calling names aloud.
  • Put prices, forms and steps in writing where they can be seen.
  • Light the counter well, with the light on the staff member's face, not behind it.
  • Train staff in the basics: face the person, do not shout, offer to write. Share our page on communicating with a Deaf person.
  • Teach front-line staff a few signs. ISLRTC's free basic ISL course names government employees, customer relation assistants and public-facing staff among the people it is meant for.

Schools and colleges

  • Seat the Deaf student near the front, with a clear view of the teacher and the board, away from the glare of windows.
  • Write key words, homework and announcements on the board. Do not talk while facing the board.
  • Share notes and slides in advance.
  • Use captioned videos, or give a written summary.
  • Arrange an interpreter where the student signs. ISLRTC publishes guidance on educational interpreting and a directory of interpreters.
  • Appoint a note-taking partner. A student cannot watch an interpreter or lip-read and write at the same time.
  • Repeat classmates' questions before answering them.
  • Teach the class some signs. Children learn fast and enjoy it.

Offices

  • Meetings: one speaker at a time; an agenda in writing; notes circulated afterwards; live captions turned on in video calls.
  • Use chat and email for instructions, not only spoken words across the room.
  • Seat the Deaf colleague where they can see the room and people approaching.
  • Budget for interpreters for interviews, training and appraisals.
  • Include them in the informal talk. Tea-break chatter is where much is learnt.

Clinics and hospitals

  • Ask at registration how the patient prefers to communicate, and note it on the file.
  • Do not use a child or a relative as interpreter for private medical matters if the patient does not want it.
  • Give instructions for medicines in writing.
  • Face the patient when wearing a mask, and write more. A mask hides the lips and half the face.
  • Come and fetch the patient when their turn comes.

Events and functions

  • Book an ISL interpreter early and place them on stage beside the speaker, well lit.
  • Reserve seats in front with a clear view of the interpreter.
  • Show captions or the text of speeches on a screen.
  • Mention in the invitation that interpretation is provided.

Safety

  • Fit flashing lights to fire alarms, or agree on a person who will alert each Deaf occupant.
  • Put up clear exit signs and evacuation maps.
  • In a drill or an emergency, tell Deaf people directly what is happening, in writing or by gesture.
  • Lifts and locked rooms: provide a way to call for help that does not depend on a voice intercom.

Remote interpreting

Where an interpreter cannot be present, video interpreting can connect one by phone or computer. ISLRTC's home page lists a Video Relay Service with weekday hours; see islrtc.nic.in for the current details.

Ask the users

Before spending money, ask Deaf employees, students or customers what gets in their way. They will name two or three things, usually cheap ones, that no checklist would have guessed.
