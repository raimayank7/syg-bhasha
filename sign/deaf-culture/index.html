Sign Language

Deaf culture and respectful words

People who share a language share more than words: they share jokes, manners, stories and a sense of belonging. Deaf people who sign are such a community. ISLRTC's basic course lists "awareness and respect for Deaf culture, etiquette, and norms" among its aims. Here is a short introduction for hearing readers.

Words to use

  • Deaf and hard of hearing are the terms most widely accepted by the community. Many people write Deaf with a capital D when they mean the community that uses sign language.
  • Sign language user or signer.
  • Hearing person for someone who is not deaf. It is a neutral word.

Words to avoid

  • "Deaf and dumb" and "deaf-mute", and in Hindi गूँगा-बहरा. They are inaccurate, since deafness does not take away the voice or the mind, and they wound. The plain word बधिर is used in Hindi.
  • "Suffering from deafness", "victim", "afflicted". Most Deaf people do not see themselves as ill.
  • "Normal" for hearing people, which makes everyone else abnormal.

You will see "hearing impaired" in official documents and in the names of institutions. Many Deaf people dislike it because it defines them by something broken. If you are unsure, use the word the person uses for themselves.

A community, not a diagnosis

To a doctor, deafness is a condition of the ear. To Deaf signers it is also membership of a community with its own language. That is why many are proud to be Deaf and do not wish to be "fixed". Both views exist, sometimes in the same family. A hearing person does not need to settle the matter, only to respect the person in front of them.

Name signs

Spelling a name letter by letter each time is slow, so signers give people name signs: one sign that stands for that person, often based on a feature, a habit or the first letter. A name sign is given to you by Deaf people. You do not invent your own.

Deaf manners

  • Eye contact is listening. Looking away while someone signs is like covering your ears.
  • Touching to get attention on the shoulder or arm is normal and polite.
  • Walking between two signers is fine if you must: go through quickly without stopping or ducking.
  • Bluntness. Signers often describe people directly, by how they look. It is description, not insult.
  • Long goodbyes. A community that meets seldom and cannot easily phone takes its time to part.
  • Light matters. Signers choose bright rooms and round tables.
  • Applause is often shown by raising and twisting both hands in the air, so that it can be seen.

Days to mark

The United Nations observes the International Day of Sign Languages on 23 September each year, during the International Week of Deaf People. ISLRTC's website shows that it celebrates Sign Language Day and the International Week of the Deaf with programmes and an ISL competition. These are good dates for a school assembly, an office session or a first class in ISL.

What Deaf people often ask of hearing people

  • Learn a little of our language.
  • Do not decide for us.
  • Do not leave us out and say "never mind".
  • Put captions and interpreters where the public is addressed.
  • Let Deaf people lead in matters about Deaf people. ISLRTC itself exists, by its own account, because of "a long struggle by the deaf community".
