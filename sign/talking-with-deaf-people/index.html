Communicating with a Deaf person

You do not need to know sign language to treat a Deaf person well. Most of what helps is ordinary good manners, adjusted for someone who listens with the eyes. These tips apply at a shop counter, in an office, in a classroom and at home.

Getting attention

  • Wave gently within the person's line of sight.
  • Tap lightly on the shoulder or upper arm if they are looking away. Never grab, and never tap the head or back.
  • Switch a light off and on once to get the attention of a group in a room.
  • Tap the table so that the vibration is felt.
  • Do not shout, and do not throw things.

First, ask how

Deaf people differ. Some sign, some lip-read well, some prefer writing, some use a hearing aid or implant and speech. Ask, by gesture or in writing, "How shall we talk?" and follow their lead. This one question prevents most awkwardness.

When you speak

  • Face the person, at their level, about an arm's length away.
  • Keep your face in the light. Do not stand with a window or lamp behind you.
  • Keep your mouth clear: no hand, cup, pen or food in front of it.
  • Speak normally. Do not shout and do not stretch your lips; both make lip-reading harder.
  • Use short, plain sentences, and say the topic first.
  • If you are not understood, say it another way instead of repeating the same words louder.
  • Do not rely on lip-reading. Many sounds look the same on the lips, and even skilled lip-readers catch only part of what is said.

Use everything you have

  • Write: a notepad, or the notes or messaging app on a phone. Typing a message and showing the screen is quick and private.
  • Point and gesture. Pointing at the thing you mean is not rude here.
  • Show numbers on fingers, a calculator or a bill.
  • Use expressions. A clear questioning face shows that a question is being asked.
  • Learn a few signs: hello, thank you, sorry, please wait, yes, no. The effort is noticed. See first signs.

With an interpreter

  • Look at and talk to the Deaf person, not the interpreter. Say "What would you like?", not "Ask her what she wants."
  • Speak at a steady pace and pause between points. Interpreting lags a few seconds behind.
  • One speaker at a time in a meeting.
  • Seat the interpreter beside the speaker, in good light, where the Deaf person can see both.
  • Share papers and slides in advance.
  • Give breaks. Interpreting is hard mental work.

In a group

Groups are the hardest place for a Deaf person, because talk jumps from mouth to mouth. Sit in a circle. Raise a hand before speaking so that the person can find the speaker. Do not carry on side conversations. Never say "I'll tell you later" or "It's nothing": being left out of the joke is the commonest hurt Deaf people report.

Things not to do

  • Do not talk about the person to their companion as if they were absent.
  • Do not pretend to have understood. Ask them to repeat or write.
  • Do not turn away or walk on while talking.
  • Do not assume that deaf means unable to read, think or decide.
  • Do not use the phrase "deaf and dumb". See words and Deaf culture.
  • Do not watch a signed conversation and comment on it; it is a private talk like any other.

In an emergency

Alarms and announcements are not heard. In a fire drill, a train delay or a hospital queue, tell the Deaf person directly, in writing or by gesture, what is happening and what to do. If you run a public place, see making a place accessible.
