Myths and facts about sign language

Most hearing people have never been taught anything about sign language, so they fill the gap with guesses. Here are ten of the commonest, each followed by the fact.

Myth 1: Sign language is the same all over the world

Fact: It is not. Sign languages grew up in their own communities, as spoken languages did. Indian Sign Language, American Sign Language and British Sign Language are different languages. A Deaf visitor from abroad must learn ISL, or find common ground through gesture, just as a hearing visitor must with Hindi.

Myth 2: ISL is Hindi or English shown with the hands

Fact: ISL has, in the words of India's national centre for the language, "distinct grammar and vocabulary". Its sentence order is its own. Signing each word of an English sentence in English order produces something a Deaf signer finds as odd as "Yesterday I market went" sounds to you.

Myth 3: Signs are just gestures and pictures in the air

Fact: Some signs look like what they mean, and that helps learners. Most do not, and none can be reliably guessed. Signs are built from a fixed set of hand shapes, places and movements, in the way that spoken words are built from a fixed set of sounds.

Myth 4: Sign language cannot express abstract or complex ideas

Fact: Anything that can be said can be signed: law, mathematics, philosophy, poetry, jokes. ISL versions of NCERT school textbooks have been developed by ISLRTC with NCERT, and news is interpreted into sign every day.

Myth 5: All Deaf people can lip-read

Fact: Lip-reading is a difficult skill and an unreliable one. Many sounds look identical on the lips, so even the best lip-readers are guessing much of the time and are worn out by long conversations. Never assume it. Ask how the person prefers to communicate.

Myth 6: Deaf people cannot speak, so they are "dumb"

Fact: Deafness affects hearing, not the voice or the mind. Many Deaf people can speak; many choose not to, because they cannot hear their own voice and signing is their natural language. The word "dumb" is both wrong and hurtful. Say "Deaf" or "hard of hearing".

Myth 7: Teaching a deaf child to sign will stop the child from learning to speak or read

Fact: What harms a child is having no language at all in the early years. A child who has a full first language, which for a deaf child can be a sign language, has a base on which to build reading, writing and, where it is possible, speech. Promoting ISL "as educational mode for deaf students" at all levels is one of ISLRTC's stated objectives. Decisions about hearing aids, implants and therapy are medical ones: take them with qualified professionals, and they do not have to exclude signing.

Myth 8: Hearing aids and implants make a deaf person hear like everyone else

Fact: These devices help many people a great deal, but they do not restore ordinary hearing, and how much they help differs from person to person. In noise, in groups and at a distance, a person with a device may still miss much of what is said.

Myth 9: Fingerspelling the alphabet is sign language

Fact: Fingerspelling is a small tool inside the language, used mainly for names. Knowing the alphabet is to ISL what knowing A to Z is to English. See fingerspelling in ISL.

Myth 10: Interpreting is easy for anyone who knows some signs

Fact: Interpreting means listening in one language and producing another at the same moment, accurately and in confidence. In India it is taught through a two-year diploma. Using a relative or a child as interpreter in a hospital, bank or police station puts the Deaf person's privacy and safety at risk. See courses and careers.

What follows from the facts

Three simple habits: treat ISL as a real language; ask each Deaf person how they prefer to communicate; and learn a little yourself. Begin with what ISL is and how to communicate with a Deaf person.
