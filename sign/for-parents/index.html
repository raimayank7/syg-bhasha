Sign Language

For parents of a deaf or hard of hearing child

Learning that your child cannot hear, or hears little, is a shock for most parents, nearly all of whom are hearing and have never met a Deaf adult. This page is a starting point from one point of view: language. It is general information, not medical advice. Decisions about tests, hearing aids, implants and therapy belong with your child's doctor and audiologist.

The one thing that cannot wait: language

A child's mind is built for language in the first years of life. A hearing baby soaks it up through the ears all day long. A deaf baby in a speaking home gets very little of it unless the family makes language visible. A child who reaches school age without a full language, signed or spoken, struggles afterwards with reading, with learning and with making friends. That, more than deafness itself, is what holds children back.

So whatever else you decide, give your child language now, in a form the child can take in fully. A sign language is fully visible. It can be started at once and at home, while medical decisions are still being made, and it does not close any other door.

"Will signing stop my child from speaking?"

This is the fear most parents carry, and many are told it by well-meaning relatives. Sign and speech are not enemies. A child with a strong first language has something to build a second on. Many deaf children grow up using both sign and speech, in different places and with different people. Discuss the plan for speech and hearing with your child's professionals, and keep signing beside it.

Learning ISL as a family

  • Start with the signs of your day: milk, eat, sleep, bath, more, finished, mother, father, come, no, love. Look them up in the official ISL Dictionary.
  • Take the free course. ISLRTC's basic ISL course in self-learning mode is free and online.
  • Everyone learns, not only the mother: father, grandparents, brothers, sisters. A child who can talk with one person in the house is still lonely.
  • Sign all day, not only in "lesson time". Narrate what you are doing, as you would speak to a hearing baby.
  • Do not wait to be fluent. Ten signs used every day are worth more than a hundred learnt and kept in a notebook.

Meet Deaf adults

Nothing eases a parent's fear like meeting Deaf adults who work, marry, raise children, joke and argue. Look for a Deaf association in your city, a school for the deaf, or a parents' group. Deaf adults are also the best signing models your child can have. ISLRTC keeps directories of schools for the deaf and of ISL teachers.

Everyday habits that help

  • Get the child's eyes before you sign or speak: a wave or a gentle touch.
  • Keep your face in the light and at the child's level.
  • Point to a thing, then sign its name, then point again.
  • Read picture books together, signing what is on the page.
  • At family gatherings, make sure someone keeps the child in the conversation.
  • Use visual signals at home: a light switch flicked for "dinner is ready".

School

Ask any school you consider how your child will follow lessons: through a teacher who signs, an interpreter, captions, or only lip-reading and guesswork. Visit a class. ISL versions of NCERT textbooks for classes I to VI have been developed by ISLRTC and NCERT and are on the DIKSHA portal, which helps with study at home.

Papers and support

A disability certificate and UDID card are needed for many government schemes, scholarships and concessions. ISLRTC's home page links to the Government's portal for applying. Rules and benefits change; read them on the official portal.

Look after yourself

Sadness, guilt and worry are normal in the first months. They ease as you and your child begin to understand each other. Your child does not need perfect parents. Your child needs to be talked with, every day, in a language the child can see.
