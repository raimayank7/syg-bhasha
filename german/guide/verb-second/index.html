German word order: the verb goes second

German word order follows a few firm rules. If you learn these three, you can build most sentences.

Rule 1: the conjugated verb is in second position

In a statement the verb stands in the second position, whatever comes first. The first position can be the subject or something else, and then the subject follows the verb:

  • "Ich lerne heute Deutsch." (I am learning German today.)
  • "Heute lerne ich Deutsch." (Today I am learning German.)
  • "Deutsch lerne ich heute." (German I am learning today.)

Rule 2: a second verb goes to the end

With a modal verb (können, müssen, wollen) or the perfect tense, the second verb part stands at the end of the clause:

  • "Ich kann heute nicht kommen." (I cannot come today.)
  • "Wir haben gestern Fußball gespielt." (We played football yesterday.)

Rule 3: in subordinate clauses the verb is last

After words such as dass (that), weil (because), wenn (if), obwohl (although), the conjugated verb goes to the end of its clause:

  • "Ich bleibe zu Hause, weil ich müde bin." (I stay at home because I am tired.)
  • "Er sagt, dass er heute nicht kommen kann."

Questions

  • Yes or no questions start with the verb: "Sprichst du Deutsch?"
  • Questions with a question word put the verb second: "Wo wohnst du?" "Was machst du?"

Time, manner, place

When several details follow the verb, the usual order is time, then manner, then place: "Ich fahre morgen mit dem Zug nach Berlin."

Try it

Take a verb from the verb tables and put it in each of the three positions.

Questions

Is the verb always second?

The conjugated verb is second in statements and in questions with a question word. In yes/no questions and commands it is first, and in subordinate clauses it goes to the end.

What does "second position" mean?

It means the second element, not the second word: "Mein Bruder aus Berlin kommt morgen" has the verb after a long first element.
