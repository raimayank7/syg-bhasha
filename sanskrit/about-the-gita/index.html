About the Bhagavad Gita: chapters, verses and setting

The Bhagavad Gita, "the song of the Lord", is a Sanskrit poem of 700 verses in 18 chapters. It forms part of the Bhishma Parva, the sixth book of the Mahabharata. Its setting is the field of Kurukshetra, just before the great battle is to begin. The warrior Arjuna, seeing his teachers and relatives drawn up on both sides, lays down his bow and refuses to fight. The rest of the poem is the conversation that follows between Arjuna and Krishna, who is serving as his charioteer.

The whole dialogue is reported to the blind king Dhritarashtra by his minister Sanjaya, which is why the poem opens with the king's question and closes with Sanjaya's words. There are thus four voices in the poem: Dhritarashtra, Sanjaya, Arjuna and Krishna, who speaks by far the greater part.

What the chapters are about

Readers commonly group the eighteen chapters in three sets of six. The first six deal mainly with action and self-discipline: the grief of Arjuna, the nature of the self, work without attachment, and meditation. The middle six turn to devotion and to the nature of the divine, reaching a climax in the vision of the universal form in the eleventh chapter. The last six analyse knowledge, the three qualities of nature (गुण), faith, and freedom, and close with the final teaching. This three-part reading is a traditional aid, not a rule; themes run across the whole book.

The language and the metre

Most of the Gita is in the anushtubh metre, the "shloka" proper, with four quarters of eight syllables. At moments of heightened feeling, such as the description of the eternal self in the second chapter and the vision of the eleventh, the poem moves into a longer metre with eleven syllables in each quarter. The Sanskrit is classical and comparatively simple, which is one reason the Gita has been a first reading text for students for centuries. Much of its vocabulary lives on in Hindi: कर्म, धर्म, योग, ज्ञान, भक्ति, आत्मा, शांति.

How the Gita is studied

Over many centuries the great teachers of the different schools of Indian philosophy wrote commentaries on the Gita, each reading it in the light of their own tradition, and they differ on important points. In modern times it has been translated into most of the world's languages. This site does not enter into those interpretations. It offers a small number of well-known verses with the plain meaning of their words, as an aid to learning Sanskrit, and leaves deeper study to teachers and to the traditional commentaries.
