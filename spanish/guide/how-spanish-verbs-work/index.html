Spanish Verbs

How Spanish verbs work: endings, persons and irregular verbs

A Spanish verb changes its ending to show who does the action and when. English has few such endings (I speak, she speaks), while Spanish has a different ending for each person, so the subject word is often left out.

Three families

Every verb in the dictionary ends in -ar (hablar), -er (comer) or -ir (vivir). Remove the ending to get the stem (habl-, com-, viv-) and add the ending for the tense. Most verbs follow the pattern of their family, and these are called regular verbs.

Six persons

  • yo (I), tú (you, informal), él / ella / usted (he / she / you, formal), nosotros (we), vosotros (you all, informal, used mainly in Spain), ellos / ellas / ustedes (they / you all).
  • In Latin America ustedes is used for "you all" in every situation, and the vosotros forms are rarely used. In some regions a form called voseo (vos) replaces tú. This site shows the standard tú and vosotros forms.

Six tenses on this site

The conjugator shows six tenses: present, preterite, imperfect, future, conditional and present subjunctive, with the gerund (hablando) and the past participle (hablado). They are enough for most everyday speech. Compound tenses such as "he hablado" use the verb haber with the participle and are not in the tables.

Irregular verbs

Some of the most common verbs are irregular: ser, estar, ir, tener, hacer, poder, querer, decir, ver, dar, saber, venir, poner and salir are all in the tool. They change their stem, or their yo form, or both. Irregular verbs are the ones to learn first because they are the most used. Other verbs also change their stem (for example pensar - pienso, dormir - duermo), and this site does not cover them yet.

Accents

Accents show which syllable is stressed and tell some words apart. They matter in verbs: hablo (I speak), habló (he spoke); hablara and hablará are different forms. Always copy them with the accent.

How we check the tables

All the forms on this site were compared with the conjugation tables of the English Wiktionary, tense by tense, in order, for all 47 verbs. See the About page.

Questions

Do I always need the subject word?

No. The ending already shows the person, so Spanish often leaves out yo, tú and so on, and uses them for emphasis or clarity.

What is the difference between tú and usted?

Tú is informal (friends, family, children). Usted is formal or respectful (strangers, elders, business), and takes the same verb form as él and ella.
