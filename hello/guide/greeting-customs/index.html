Greeting customs: how people say hello with words and hands

A greeting is more than a word. In most places it is a small ceremony of words, a gesture and a way of addressing the person, and getting it right shows that you have taken the trouble to learn. This page describes the greeting customs of the places whose languages are on this site. It describes common practice; families and regions vary, and you should follow the lead of the person you meet.

India

The traditional Indian greeting is namaste (and its equivalents such as vanakkam and sat sri akaal), said with the palms pressed together in front of the chest and a slight nod. It is suitable for any person at any time of day. Elders are often greeted by touching their feet or their knees; younger people may do this at a festival or a family meeting, and a visitor is not expected to. Handshakes are common in cities and among men. Between a man and a woman who do not know each other, wait for the woman to offer her hand, or simply use namaste. Adding ji to a name or a title shows respect.

Japan and Korea

In Japan the greeting is a bow, and the depth shows the respect: a slight nod among friends, about thirty degrees to a customer or an elder, deeper for a formal apology or thanks. In Korea people also bow, often with a handshake in business, using both hands or supporting the right arm with the left as a sign of respect. In both countries people often use the surname with a title, and first names are used only among close friends.

Thailand and Southeast Asia

The Thai wai places the palms together near the face or chest and bows the head slightly. The higher the hands, the greater the respect, and the younger person usually bows first. In Indonesia and Malaysia, a light touch of the right hand to the heart after a handshake is common, and the left hand is not used for giving or taking things.

The Arab world and Turkey

People greet with assalamu alaikum and a long exchange of enquiries about health and family. Men often shake hands and may add a touch on the shoulder or cheek, while the greeting between men and women depends on the family and the country, so it is courteous to wait for the other person to offer a hand. A guest is nearly always offered coffee or tea, and the first refusal is often part of the politeness.

Europe and the Americas

A handshake with eye contact is normal in Germany, Russia and most of northern Europe. In France, Spain, Italy, Portugal and Latin America, friends greet with a kiss on the cheek, the number and side differing from place to place. In Russia a man should take off a glove before a handshake. In all of these places, using a title and the polite form of "you" with a stranger is the safe choice.

Mistakes to avoid

  • Do not use a first name or a casual form with a person older than you until they invite you.
  • Do not assume that a gesture which is friendly at home has the same meaning elsewhere. The thumbs-up, the "OK" circle and pointing with a finger can be rude in some places.
  • Do not rush. A greeting is part of the business, and skipping it can be taken as cold.

Questions

What is the safest greeting when I am unsure?

A smile, a slight nod, and the polite spoken greeting of the language. Let the other person decide whether to shake hands, bow or hug.

Should visitors touch the feet of elders in India?

It is not expected of a visitor. A respectful namaste is enough.
