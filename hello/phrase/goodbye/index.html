Goodbye is a harder word than hello, because many languages prefer a hopeful goodbye to a final one. The table gives the standard form in each language, but a few of them come with a special custom that is worth knowing.

"I will go and come back"

In Tamil, Telugu, Kannada, Malayalam and Marathi the usual way to leave is to say, in effect, "I will go and come back": the Tamil poittu varen, Telugu velloostanu, Kannada hogi baruttene, Malayalam poyi varam, Marathi yeto. The farewell avoids any sense of an ending, and the other person replies with something like "go and come". The Gujarati aavjo means "do come again". This makes the final goodbye, such as the Hindi alvida, a word for very long partings.

Final and casual goodbyes

The Hindi alvida and the Japanese sayounara are kept for long separations and can sound dramatic. In daily life Hindi speakers say phir milenge (we shall meet again) or just chalo, and Japanese speakers say mata ne or jaa ne. The Spanish adiós, from "to God", is a general goodbye, and hasta luego (until later) is the everyday one. The French au revoir and German auf Wiedersehen both literally mean "until we see again". Many of these go back to the same hope.

Two goodbyes in Korean

Korean has a different goodbye for the one who leaves and the one who stays: annyeonghi gaseyo (go in peace) to the one leaving, annyeonghi gyeseyo (stay in peace) when you leave. Turkish has the same split with güle güle and hoşça kal. If you say the wrong one, you will still be understood, but a speaker will smile at the slip.

Questions

Why do South Indian languages not say goodbye?

Because a final goodbye can feel unlucky, people say they will go and come back. It is a courtesy, not a promise to return.

What is the most universal goodbye?

A wave and the local word for bye. "Bye" itself is understood in almost every city in these countries.
