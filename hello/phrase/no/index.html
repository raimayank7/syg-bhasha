No is as useful as yes, and often more difficult to say politely. The table gives the plain word for no in each language. Many cultures prefer a softer form, and the notes below show how people say no without causing offence.

Soft ways to refuse

In Japan, a direct iie is rare in daily life; a speaker says chotto... (a little...) and lets the sentence trail off, or muzukashii desu (it is difficult). In India, "I will see" or "we will try" often means no. In Korea, aniyo is used for no, but a request is more often declined with an apology. In Arabic-speaking countries, an offer of food or tea is declined once or twice before the guest finally accepts, so a first no is part of the courtesy.

No with two meanings

In Hindi, nahin means no and is also "not" in a sentence. In Punjabi, nahin and na work the same way. Tamil has illai for "there is not" and "no", and Telugu has kaadu for "it is not" and ledu for "there is none". Arabic la means no, and also "do not". These are single words with several jobs.

Be careful with gestures

Shaking the head from side to side means no in most places, but in Bulgaria the gestures are reversed, and in India a wobble can mean agreement. Spoken words are the safest. When you must refuse and want to be kind, say no with a reason or a thank you: "no, thank you" is understood and welcomed in every language in the table.

Questions

How do I say no politely in Hindi?

"Nahin, dhanyavaad" (no, thank you) or "abhi nahin" (not now). Adding a thanks makes it gentle.

Why is no hard to say in Japan?

Because a direct refusal can hurt the other person's feelings, and people prefer to hint that something is difficult.
