Hello is the first word anyone learns in a new language, and it is also the one that matters most. A greeting tells the other person you have noticed them and wish them well, and in many places skipping it is rude. The table shows the common greeting of each language, written in its own script, with a sound spelling you can read aloud. Capital letters in the sound spelling mark the syllable to stress.

Greetings that are not just "hello"

Several of these greetings are older than the idea of saying hello at all. The Hindi and Marathi namaste and namaskar mean "I bow to you". The Punjabi sat sri akaal is a Sikh greeting that praises the timeless one as truth. The Arabic and Urdu assalamu alaikum means "peace be upon you", and it is answered with the same wish returned. The Tamil vanakkam and the Telugu, Kannada and Malayalam namaskara are bows in words. The Swahili hujambo means "do you have no troubles?", answered with sijambo, "I have none". The Russian zdravstvuyte means "be healthy". A greeting is nearly always a small blessing.

Formal and friendly forms

Most languages here have a polite greeting and a casual one. French has bonjour and salut, Italian buongiorno and ciao, Russian zdravstvuyte and privet, Korean annyeonghaseyo and annyeong. With a stranger, an elder or someone in a shop, take the polite form. With friends your own age the casual one is natural, and a polite form would sound stiff. When in doubt, choose the polite greeting; no one is offended by being treated with respect.

Greeting with the body

Words are often joined by a gesture. Namaste and vanakkam are said with the palms joined. Thai has the wai, Japanese a small bow, and Korean a bow with the head. In the Arab world a hand may be placed on the heart. A handshake is common in Europe, but in many conservative settings it is better to wait and see whether the other person offers a hand, especially across genders.

Questions

What is the most useful greeting if I do not know the language?

A smile and a plain "hello" are understood almost everywhere. If you can learn only one local word, learn the local hello: it is the one people notice and appreciate.

Why does one language have several greetings?

Greetings depend on time of day, religion, region and how well people know each other. Pick the polite everyday form from the table, and copy what the other person says if they greet you first.
