Thank you is the second word a traveller needs after hello, and one of the most searched phrases in any language. The table gives the standard polite form in 26 languages, in the writing of that language, with a sound spelling to read aloud.

Where the words come from

Many words for thank you are older words for thanks, praise or blessing. The Hindi dhanyavaad means "blessed" in Sanskrit, while shukriya is the Persian-Arabic word for gratitude, shared with Urdu. The Spanish gracias, Italian grazie and the Latin root gratia mean "grace". The French merci comes from a word for mercy. The German danke is related to "think". The Swahili asante is, like the Arabic shukran, a plain word of thanks. The Turkish teşekkür ederim is built on the Arabic shukr, so Turkish, Urdu and Arabic share a root.

Levels of politeness

Japanese has a ladder: arigatou (casual), arigatou gozaimasu (polite), and arigatou gozaimashita (polite, for something already done). Korean has gomawo for friends and gamsahamnida for everyone else. In Hindi, shukriya is the warm everyday word and dhanyavaad the formal one, found in speeches and letters. In English-speaking offices in India, "thanks" and "thank you" are used freely, in the middle of Hindi or other languages.

Do you need to say it?

The custom differs. In India, close family usually do not say thank you for everyday help, because the help is a duty of love, and a spoken thanks can even feel distant. In Britain and in service settings, thanks is said constantly. In Japan, thanks is said and often with a bow. Thanking a shopkeeper, a driver or a waiter is welcome in all the languages shown here, and is never wrong.

Questions

What is the reply to thank you?

Common replies: Hindi "koi baat nahin" or "swagat hai", Spanish "de nada", French "de rien", German "bitte", Japanese "dou itashimashite", Arabic "afwan". Many people just smile.

Is shukriya or dhanyavaad better?

Both are correct. Shukriya is warmer and more common in speech, dhanyavaad is more formal and written.
