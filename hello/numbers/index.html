Counting is the key to prices and phone numbers

The numbers one to ten are the best next step after hello and thank you. With them you can understand prices in a market, give a phone number, read a bus number and tell the time. Many languages build every larger number from these ten: in Chinese, eleven is "ten one" (shí yī) and twenty is "two ten" (èr shí), which makes counting to a hundred easy once you know ten words. Japanese and Korean do the same with their Chinese-derived numbers, and Korean also has a second set of native numbers for counting objects and giving ages.

Things to notice

  • Shared roots. The Hindi, Punjabi, Urdu, Marathi and Gujarati words for four (chaar) and seven (saat) are almost the same, because the languages are related, and the Spanish, Italian, Portuguese and French words for seven (siete, sette, sete, sept) come from one Latin word.
  • Two readings. Japanese four and seven have two readings each (shi or yon, shichi or nana), and nine has two (kyuu or ku). People choose by context: four (shi) sounds like the word for death, so yon is often preferred.
  • Tones. The Chinese, Thai and Vietnamese numbers need the right tone. A speaker can teach you the difference between sì (four) and shí (ten) in a minute.
